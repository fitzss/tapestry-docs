## 1. Introduction: A New Mode of Explanation

**Counterfactuals** are central to this perspective:

> “Counterfactual explanations of the world are explanations about what physical events could or could not be made to happen.”

Rather than focusing on what *does* happen or *is likely* to happen, we turn our attention to *what is possible or impossible* given certain constraints. This shift from “factual” to “counterfactual” is not merely a philosophical exercise; it reorients our understanding of **physics, science, and problem-solving** around tasks and transformations, rather than initial conditions evolving over time.

---

## 2. The Science of Can and Can’t

> “The Science of Can and Can’t sets out in nontechnical terms a new, counterfactual mode of explanation based on scientific and philosophical ideas that the author, Chiara Marletto, and I have pioneered.”

**The science of can and can’t** recasts physical laws in terms of *possible vs. impossible* transformations, which is a stark contrast to the traditional approach, where one might say:

> “The traditional conception of physics... can predict what the state will be at a later time... However, a statement of this kind does not tell us anything about what transformations are possible or impossible on the object.”

In other words, standard physics tells us “given X, Y will happen,” but not “given constraints A, could we achieve transformation B at all?” **The science of can and can’t** addresses that gap.

### 2.1 Why a New Mode?
The passages hint that:

> “In the past few decades, in contrast, there have been none—no new mode of explanation about physical phenomena has been discovered. Not only can counterfactuals provide new explanations; they are the foundation of a new mode of explanation.”

This suggests that despite immense scientific progress, wholly new foundational perspectives remain rare. Yet, **counterfactual-based reasoning**—focusing on tasks and their feasibility—promises to deepen and unify how we describe nature.

## 3. Constructor Theory: A Meta-Theory of Tasks and Transformations

**Constructor theory** is posited as a **“meta-theory with its own physical principles that are intended to supplement and constrain dynamical theories”** (like quantum mechanics or general relativity):

> “Constructor Theory forms Principles about counterfactuals that are stated by saying what tasks are possible, what are impossible, and why.”

### 3.1 Tasks, Substrates, and Attributes
A fundamental notion:

> “A task is a specified physical transformation on a substrate, which involves changing an attribute from one state to another.”

For instance, “flipping a bit,” “copying information,” or “lifting a mass from height 0 to height H.” Each *substrate* (like a quantum state or a physical object) has *attributes* (mass, spin, etc.), and a **constructor** is:

> “A constructor for a task T is a system which... whenever presented with the substrate of the task T in one of the input attributes, it delivers it in one of the states of the allowed output attributes, and retains the ability to do that again.”

In short, a constructor repeatedly performs a transformation without degrading. By focusing on tasks and whether they can or cannot happen, constructor theory **abstracts away** the details of specific constructors or dynamical paths. As stated:

> “Constructor-theoretic statements never refer to specific constructors, only to the fact that tasks are possible or impossible. This is what allows them to be scale and dynamics independent.”

### 3.2 Possible vs. Impossible
Constructor theory states:

> “A task is impossible if the laws of physics impose a limit on how accurately it can be performed by a constructor. Otherwise, the task is possible.”

Thus, a central question becomes: *is a transformation feasible under the given fundamental laws?* If yes, we say it is possible; if no, it is impossible—irrespective of whether we have built a constructor for it yet. We might eventually build one (or prove we cannot).

## 4. Counterfactuals: The Foundation for New Explanations

> “Counterfactual explanations of the world... are facts about what could or could not be. They are the foundation of a new mode of explanation which can provide new explanations.”

**Counterfactual statements** do not merely say, “object A will move here in 5 seconds.” They say, “Had we had enough torque, object A *could* have been moved to position P.” This perspective clarifies *why* something does not occur—and how changing constraints might allow it.

### 4.1 Factual vs. Counterfactual
- **Factual**: “Given initial condition X, system evolves to Y with probability Z.”  
- **Counterfactual**: “If we had changed X to X’, we *could* (or *could not*) achieve transformation T. *Why or why not?*”

Hence, in **constructor theory**: it’s not only about enumerating possible states, but **describing which tasks are feasible**—a more direct handle on “can or can’t.”

## 5. Physics Beyond Predictions: Emphasizing Transformations

> “Physics aims at solving problems; as a consequence, it seeks, if possible, universal and exact testable laws, formulated in whatever mode of explanation happens to be appropriate.”

The **traditional conception** of physics can be extremely powerful for predictions, but:

> “The traditional conception of physics... takes for granted that scientific theories can only be about what must happen in the universe (or is likely to), given what has happened. But none of that is true. It is no more than an arbitrary limitation on our ability to understand the world. Incorporating counterfactuals on an equal footing with factual statements into fundamental physics.”

That addition—treating tasks, transformations, and constraints as the basic objects—could unify aspects of quantum mechanics, thermodynamics, classical mechanics, or general relativity under one conceptual umbrella of feasibility. As the text suggests, if **all** physical laws can be restated as “which tasks are possible or impossible, and why,” we gain a simpler, more universal vantage point.

## 6. The Science of Can and Can’t in Practice

**Key principle**: Focus on transformations (like “copying information” or “lifting a 10 kg mass”) and check if laws (energy, quantum, no-cloning) forbid or allow them. This approach:

1. Clarifies *why* a transformation fails.  
2. Proposes *what could be changed* to make it feasible (like adding resources, rearranging configurations, or reducing errors).  
3. Potentially fosters new breakthroughs or unifications in physics, since many “gaps” in existing theory revolve around whether certain transformations can or cannot occur (especially in quantum, cosmology, or advanced materials science).

## 7. Knowledge, Universal Constructors, and Abstract Constructors

The text also addresses the role of **knowledge**:

> “Constructor theory is all down to knowledge ultimately, and same with epistemology, and same with everything.”

From this standpoint, knowledge itself is an **“abstract constructor,”** enabling transformations in the physical world. For instance, if you possess the knowledge to build a certain apparatus, you can transform raw materials into advanced devices—meaning tasks that were once *impossible* become *possible* after you embed that knowledge in your system. Similarly:

> “From the constructor-theoretic perspective, physics is almost entirely the theory of the effects that knowledge (abstract constructors) can have on the physical world, via people.”

Thus, knowledge is not just “facts about the universe,” but an active element: it changes what tasks can be performed. By tying knowledge to transformations, constructor theory unifies **epistemology** (the study of knowledge) with the physical domain.

## 8. Why This Perspective Is Novel

> “Though the overall rate of scientific discovery has greatly increased by almost any measure, the discovery of fundamentally new truths about nature has, paradoxically, become less frequent... Not only can counterfactuals provide new explanations; they are the foundation of a new mode of explanation.”

**Constructor theory** and **The Science of Can and Can’t** represent a possible next step in fundamental physics—a “new mode of explanation” that might solve or clarify problems overlooked by purely state-based theories or classical approaches. This includes:

- **Quantum No-Cloning**: Explaining why certain transformations (like duplicating an unknown quantum state) are impossible.  
- **Interoperability of info media**: Asking which “flip” or “copy” transformations are feasible in classical vs. quantum mediums.  
- **Scale Independence**: Because tasks are described in an abstract sense, the same statements can apply from nano-devices to astrophysical objects, bridging many scales elegantly.

## 9. Conclusion: The Unified Vision

Combining **counterfactuals**—“*statements about what physical events could or could not be made to happen*”—with a meta-theory that says “**all laws of physics are stated by saying what tasks are possible, what are impossible, and why**” yields a perspective that:

1. **Clarifies** the difference between purely predictive statements and fundamental constraints.  
2. **Unifies** knowledge and physical transformations, showing how intelligence (or knowledge) directly changes feasibility space.  
3. **Extends** or *supplements* existing theories (quantum, classical, relativistic) without discarding them, describing them from a vantage point of transformations rather than states.

Thus, **Constructor Theory, The Science of Can and Can’t, and Counterfactual Explanations** converge into a powerful explanatory framework—one that might *fundamentally reorient* how we talk about physics, computing, and the nature of knowledge. As the text notes:

> “A daring speculation is therefore that all the laws of physics could be formulated solely in terms of principles about counterfactuals, and that the laws of motion follow from them as derivative, and perhaps approximate, properties. Exploring this possibility is the start of an exciting research programme.”

In short, **“can or can’t”** stands at the heart of the next big leap in fundamental understanding—both of the universe’s structure and of our capacity to harness it through knowledge and constructor-based transformations.
